So your recollection is correct: **only 44 of the 750 original Baseline applications actually reach a final offer**, while another 139 are pushed into review.

That is a surprisingly narrow approval funnel for the kind of synthetic MSBF portfolio we were trying to construct.

## What is driving it

The most important observation is that the low offer rate is not primarily coming from fraud, data confidence, economics, or acquisition controls. It is overwhelmingly being driven by the **capacity/affordability policy boundary**.

In Baseline:

- `GATE_06_CAPACITY` has **407 FAIL**, **82 REVIEW**, 101 PASS and 160 BLOCKED.
- `GATE_07_AFFORDABILITY` has the **same 407 FAIL / 82 REVIEW / 101 PASS / 160 BLOCKED distribution**.

That is extremely consequential. More than half the initial population—**407 of 750, or 54.3%**—is failing the capacity/affordability layer outright before considering the other review gates.

By comparison:

- integrated-risk produces **no outright FAILs**;
- fraud produces **32 reviews but no FAILs**;
- processor continuity produces **154 reviews but no FAILs**;
- data confidence produces only **42 reviews**;
- unit economics has **159 FAILs**, but many of those applications are already failing capacity;
- upstream hard stop accounts for **117 FAILs**, again with substantial overlap.

This tells me something important about the architecture: **we built a portfolio where capacity is functioning less like one underwriting dimension and more like a dominant approval choke point.**

That does not make the rule mathematically wrong. It makes the calibration questionable.

## The review population deserves separate scrutiny

The 139 Baseline manual-review cases are also revealing.

Among those 139 applications:

- **101** have verification review;
- **82** have capacity review;
- **82** have affordability review;
- **24** have processor-continuity review;
- **16** have economics review;
- only **7** have data-confidence review;
- only **1** has fraud review;
- **6** have acquisition-evidence review.

There is overlap, so those counts do not add to 139.

The important part is that the review population is not simply a collection of obviously problematic merchants. A large fraction reaches review because the architecture says that **any review-level gate prevents straight-through eligibility**, even when the application otherwise has sufficient evidence and no fail or blocked condition.

The accepted M2.1 rule is essentially:

```text
Any FAIL       → DECLINE_POLICY
else any BLOCKED → INSUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE
else any REVIEW  → MANUAL_REVIEW
else             → ELIGIBLE_FOR_OFFER_DESIGN
```

That is clean governance logic. It is also very conservative.

For a production-style underwriting workflow, some review gates may indeed need to be binding. But for this MSBF design, I am increasingly unconvinced that **every review classification should have equal veto power over automatic offer design**.

Processor continuity review, verification review, marginal capacity, economics review, acquisition-evidence review and fraud review do not necessarily represent the same kind of risk or require the same routing consequence.

## Merchant 000003 exposes a second calibration issue

Its Baseline economics are actually reasonably attractive:

- requested amount: $61,300;
- average eligible sales: about $4,035/day;
- integrated risk: Tier 2 / moderate;
- comparative loss: about 6.37%;
- risk-adjusted contribution: about $2,168;
- annualized risk-adjusted return: about 21.5%;
- economics: above hurdle.

Yet it receives a policy decline because the **requested structure** produces:

- payment coverage around **0.73x**;
- negative residual daily operating cash flow.

I agree that we should not fund *that requested structure*.

Where I am less comfortable is the leap from:

> “This requested amount is unaffordable”

to:

> “No supportable structure exists; policy decline.”

That is probably the more important design question than whether 000003 technically followed the current routing rules.

For an MSBF product, a merchant requesting too much capital should often be a natural **counteroffer candidate**, provided there is a smaller structure that satisfies capacity, risk, and economics.

And our architecture already acknowledges that concept: M2.2 contains capacity-aligned and conservative counteroffer templates.

The potential design problem is that M2.1 may be **declining too early**, before M2.2 gets a meaningful opportunity to find a supportable reduced structure.

In other words, the workflow currently behaves roughly like:

```text
Requested structure fails capacity
        ↓
DECLINE_POLICY
        ↓
M2.2 is not allowed to search for a structure
```

Where a more commercially realistic architecture could be:

```text
Requested structure fails capacity
        ↓
Is there sufficient evidence to determine sustainable capacity?
        ↓
YES
        ↓
Route to structure search / counteroffer
        ↓
If a compliant reduced structure exists → REVIEW or OFFER
If none exists                         → DECLINE_POLICY
```

That is a materially different philosophy.

## That may explain both symptoms you noticed

Your two observations—

1. **very few approvals**, and
2. **a large review population**

—may actually have the same underlying cause: M2.1 is attempting to make too much of the final credit decision **before structure optimization occurs**.

The original architecture is highly gate-centric:

```text
Application
→ policy gates
→ route
→ structure generation
→ final decision
```

For a merchant cash-advance / sales-based financing product, I think the more natural architecture is closer to:

```text
Application evidence
→ hard eligibility / evidence gates
→ determine sustainable capacity
→ generate feasible structure
→ evaluate risk/economics at that structure
→ authorize / review / decline
```

The difference is subtle but fundamental.

Capacity should certainly constrain the offer. I am not sure it should always **decline the merchant simply because the merchant's requested amount is too large**.

## What I would preserve

I would **not** loosen everything simply to manufacture a prettier approval rate.

Several current principles are strong and should remain:

- hard verification failures should remain hard stops;
- genuinely insufficient evidence should remain distinct from adverse credit conclusions;
- missing evidence should never be converted to zero;
- stress should not create an implausible improvement relative to Baseline;
- risk and economics should still constrain a proposed structure;
- manual review should remain available where uncertainty genuinely requires judgment;
- an offer should not be authorized merely to hit an arbitrary target approval rate.

So I would not say, “We only approved 5.9%, therefore make the rules easier.”

I would say:

> **A 5.9% straight-through offer rate is a diagnostic signal that warrants recalibration review, especially because the dominant rejection mechanism is requested-structure capacity rather than fundamental merchant ineligibility.**

That is a much stronger and more defensible conclusion.

## My current view of the routing philosophy

I would divide the existing M2.1 gates into three conceptual classes.

| Gate type | Examples | Likely appropriate routing role |
|---|---|---|
| **True eligibility / hard-stop gates** | fraud hard stop, bank verification mismatch, unusable evidence | May legitimately prevent structure generation |
| **Structure-constraining gates** | capacity, affordability, some economics | Should often constrain or resize the offer before causing decline |
| **Judgment / monitoring gates** | processor continuity, moderate verification concerns, some risk/economic review states | May warrant review, but should not automatically behave identically |

Right now the architecture largely collapses the latter two categories into route severity.

That is the part I would revisit.

## So, specifically on Merchant 000003

I would now phrase my conclusion more carefully:

> **Merchant 000003's accepted final outcome is correctly generated under the accepted M2 routing rules, but I am not yet persuaded that those routing rules embody the best intended underwriting design. In particular, the Baseline evidence supports rejecting the requested $61,300 structure, but it does not necessarily establish that the merchant should receive no financing structure at all.**

That is different from saying the existing decision is wrong.

We have not yet run the counterfactual structure search needed to determine whether a smaller amount would have satisfied the existing capacity, risk, and economics framework. We should do that before deciding whether 000003 itself should have been a counteroffer rather than a decline.

## What I recommend before remediation

Before modifying M1.15/M2.1, I think we should add **one bounded policy-calibration diagnostic** to the remediation analysis.

We should take the 750 Baseline applications and answer three questions:

1. Of the **407 capacity/affordability FAILs**, how many have enough evidence and otherwise acceptable risk/economics that a smaller structure could satisfy the existing methodology?
2. Of the **139 manual-review cases**, which particular review gates are actually binding, and how many would become eligible if we distinguish “structure review” or “operational monitoring” from true underwriting review?
3. What would the outcome distribution look like under several **rule-preserving counterfactuals**, without changing any thresholds—for example, allowing capacity FAIL at the requested amount to enter M2.2 structure search rather than immediately becoming a policy decline?

That last point is especially useful because it lets us determine whether the problem is **threshold calibration** or **routing architecture**.

My working hypothesis is that a significant portion of the 69.9% Baseline policy-decline population does not require looser credit standards. It may require allowing the existing structure engine to do the job it was designed to do: **find an affordable counteroffer before declaring that no supportable structure exists.**

If that hypothesis holds, I would favor correcting the routing architecture rather than simply relaxing capacity thresholds.
