I would frame the future state as a bank-grade Merchant Sales-Based Financing Portfolio Intelligence platform, not simply “more Power BI tabs.” The impressive part for senior credit-risk leaders is the analytical progression: origination strategy → cohort formation → longitudinal performance → risk migration → servicing/remediation → comparative strategy intelligence → executive portfolio oversight.

The roadmap below deliberately stays at the product/concept level rather than prescribing layouts.

Preliminary Future Roadmap

Merchant Sales-Based Financing — Credit Risk & Portfolio Intelligence

Product vision

Build an end-to-end analytical environment that allows a credit-risk leader to move seamlessly from how a financing cohort was created, to how it performs through time, to why performance changes, to which populations require attention, and ultimately to whether newer strategies are producing better risk-adjusted outcomes than prior cohorts.

The governing analytical principle should be:

Preserve the population, preserve the decision context, preserve time, and make every movement explainable.

The current synthetic run becomes the first demonstrable cohort. Future expansion would allow leaders to select and compare many governed cohorts without losing the lineage connecting origination decisions to subsequent portfolio outcomes.

P01 — Cohort Command Center

What is this cohort, and where does it stand now?

This remains the executive entry point.

A leader selects a cohort/scenario and observation date and receives a concise view of its origination profile, current condition, concentrations, risk/economics, review signals, and governance state.

It should answer questions such as:

What did we originate?
How much was funded?
What was the original risk/economic posture?
How much exposure remains?
Is remittance performance on pace?
Where are concentrations?
Are servicing or risk signals emerging?

This is the point-in-time executive cockpit.

P02 — Cohort Lifecycle

How did this cohort get here?

This is the longitudinal complement to P01.

Instead of looking at one observation date, the leader sees the complete performance trajectory from funding through monitoring and eventual endpoint.

It should convey the evolution of:

  • exposure;
  • remittance performance;
  • active alerts;
  • servicing workload;
  • payoff timing/slippage;
  • payoff progression.

Scenario comparison provides context for whether observed behavior is relatively benign or stressed.

The fundamental question becomes:

Where did this cohort begin to diverge from its expected path, and what happened afterward?

This is particularly appropriate for sales-based financing because the underlying performance framework is high-frequency rather than primarily conventional 30/60/90 DPD. [export reference: filecite | turn27file13 | L688-L727]

P03 — Cohort Comparison

Are newer cohorts getting better or worse?

This is where the platform begins to become significantly more powerful.

Rather than evaluating cohorts independently, leaders compare multiple origination populations across consistent lifecycle intervals.

The key innovation should eventually be cohort age:

Day 1 → Day 7 → Day 14 → Day 30 → Day 60 → endpoint

rather than relying exclusively on calendar date.

That enables apples-to-apples questions:

Is the August cohort outperforming July at Day 30?
Did the new policy improve early remittance behavior?
Are newer cohorts paying down faster?
Did expected loss improve at the cost of approval or funding volume?
Is servicing burden appearing earlier in newer vintages?

This becomes the platform's vintage intelligence layer.

P04 — Origination Strategy & Decision Intelligence

Why did we originate this portfolio?

This page moves upstream.

It should explain the credit funnel and decision architecture that produced each cohort:

Application → policy evaluation → counteroffer/review → final authorization → booking → funding.

Executives should be able to understand not merely approval/decline counts but which policy and risk conditions drove the resulting portfolio composition.

Over time, this becomes particularly valuable for comparing strategy versions:

What changed when Policy V3 replaced Policy V2?
Did approval improve because of genuine risk selection or simply greater risk tolerance?
Did counteroffers preserve acceptable economics?
Which rules are responsible for the largest volume loss?

The current decision-outcome framework already provides the foundation for this analysis.

P05 — Risk & Economics

Are we being compensated for the risk we are taking?

This should become the deeper analytical destination for the risk/economics concepts currently summarized on P01.

The focus is the relationship among:

volume, expected loss, contribution, exposure, industry, acquisition source, risk characteristics and financing structure.

It should allow executives to identify:

profitable-looking but risk-heavy concentrations;
high-volume segments with weak economics;
attractive risk-adjusted populations;
segments producing negative contribution;
differences between Baseline and stressed outcomes.

Critically, the language remains bounded: comparative synthetic expected loss and economics—not CECL, audited profitability or regulatory reserve conclusions.

P06 — Performance Migration & Early Warning

Where is deterioration emerging before it becomes severe?

This is where the high-frequency MSBF monitoring framework can distinguish the project from a conventional credit dashboard.

Instead of simply reporting delinquency buckets, it would examine migration among states such as:

Current → Watch → Underperforming → Severe Shortfall → Paid Off

and the drivers behind those migrations.

The underlying framework already recognizes that low sales with proportional remittance is analytically different from sales occurring while required remittance disappears, and both differ from complete disappearance of sales and remittance. [export reference: filecite | turn27file13 | L722-L727]

That distinction is valuable credit-risk analytics.

The executive question becomes:

Who is deteriorating, how quickly, and what kind of deterioration is it?

P07 — Servicing & Remediation Intelligence

Where should analytical attention be concentrated?

This could become particularly relevant to the remediation/analytics audience you mentioned.

The page should connect risk deterioration to operational workload:

  • open servicing population;
  • active alerts;
  • payment/remittance exceptions;
  • payoff slippage;
  • unresolved conditions;
  • reassessment needs;
  • concentration of workload by cohort, industry or acquisition channel.

The analytical objective is not autonomous action.

It is:

Which populations are creating servicing burden, what conditions are driving that burden, and how is it changing?

Eventually, this could support retrospective evaluation of remediation strategies:

Which interventions were associated with stabilization?
Which populations repeatedly re-entered review?
Where did operational effort concentrate relative to exposure?

That creates a natural bridge between credit risk analytics and remediation analytics.

P08 — Concentration & Portfolio Mix

Where are we accumulating correlated exposure?

Once multiple cohorts exist, executives need a view across them rather than only within them.

This page would examine portfolio composition across:

  • industry;
  • acquisition source;
  • product/strategy;
  • risk tier;
  • cohort/vintage;
  • potentially geography and other governed dimensions.

The key distinction from P01 is that this becomes a cross-cohort portfolio view.

It answers:

Are individually acceptable cohorts collectively creating an undesirable concentration?

P09 — Current Portfolio

What does the entire active book look like today?

This is the conventional portfolio view that we originally thought P01 represented.

Rather than fixing a cohort, this page intentionally combines all relevant active cohorts at a selected calendar date.

It becomes the executive answer to:

How much exposure do we have today?
How many merchants/accounts remain active?
What is aggregate performance?
Where is deterioration concentrated?
How much servicing burden exists?
Which vintages contribute most to today's risk?

The distinction would be explicit:

Cohort pages follow a fixed population through time.

Current Portfolio follows the changing book at a fixed point in calendar time.

That is an analytically mature separation.

P10 — Strategy Backtesting & Champion/Challenger

Would another strategy have produced a better portfolio?

This is a more advanced future state and potentially one of the most impressive components.

Using governed synthetic scenarios and deterministic decision logic, compare alternative strategies against the same underlying application population.

The analysis could examine tradeoffs among:

authorization rate ↔ funding volume ↔ expected loss ↔ contribution ↔ subsequent performance ↔ servicing burden.

Instead of saying:

“Strategy B approves more.”

the system could eventually say:

“Strategy B increases authorized volume, but the incremental population produces materially different expected-loss, remittance and servicing trajectories.”

That's much closer to how senior credit strategy teams actually need to evaluate policy changes.

P11 — Executive Portfolio Intelligence

What changed, why does it matter, and where should I investigate?

This would eventually become the senior-most management layer.

Rather than another dense dashboard, it should synthesize the strongest signals from the underlying pages:

Portfolio posture
Cohort movement
Risk migration
Concentrations
Economics
Servicing/remediation burden
Strategy changes

The objective is not to manufacture recommendations. It is to direct executive attention toward material, explainable changes supported by governed evidence.

A leader should be able to start here and then move naturally into the appropriate analytical layer.

Longer-term analytical evolution

The most important architectural progression is not actually the number of pages. It is the evolution of the cohort construct.

Stage 1 — Current state
One governed run behaves as one fixed cohort.

Stage 2 — Cohort selection
Multiple governed cohorts become selectable.

Stage 3 — Cohort-age normalization
Different vintages can be compared at equivalent lifecycle ages.

Stage 4 — Cohort comparison
Compare origination strategies, channels, industries and vintages.

Stage 5 — Current portfolio aggregation
Combine active cohorts into the current book while preserving cohort lineage.

Stage 6 — Strategy/performance linkage
Trace downstream performance back to the exact origination strategy that produced it.

Stage 7 — Controlled strategy simulation
Evaluate alternative strategy outcomes against governed populations without confusing simulation with production decisions.

That final lineage is the compelling part:

What we decided → what we funded → how it performed → where it deteriorated → what operational burden resulted → whether a different strategy would have produced a better risk-adjusted outcome.

Why this can stand out

The differentiator should not be “a sophisticated Power BI dashboard.” Banking leaders see sophisticated dashboards routinely.

The stronger story is that this becomes a traceable credit-risk decision and portfolio intelligence architecture in which underwriting, strategy, cohort formation, longitudinal performance, early warning, servicing/remediation and economics remain analytically connected.

And importantly, the project already contains many of the semantic building blocks rather than this being purely aspirational. The accepted model includes measures for funding, exposure, cumulative actual/expected remittance, fulfillment, payoff slippage, alerts, decision outcomes, expected loss, risk-adjusted contribution, negative contribution, servicing queues and reconciliation. Assistant · C109-M00239 · 2026-08-26T11:27:44.204124-04:00 · active path

Yes. There are a few ways to reduce this substantially, although Power BI doesn't have a universal “stop recalculating while I edit the report” switch.

For what you're doing right now, the most useful option is Pause visuals.

In Power BI Desktop, look on the ribbon for:

Optimize → Pause visuals

Turn that on while you're doing repetitive construction work—renaming fields for visuals, repositioning objects, formatting, changing titles, etc. Power BI will stop continuously issuing queries to refresh visuals after every little change.

Then when you're ready to inspect the page:

Optimize → Refresh visuals

or resume visual queries.

That is exactly what I would use while we're finishing P02.

A second feature worth enabling is Query reduction. Under:

File → Options and settings → Options → Query reduction

Power BI can add an Apply button to slicers/filter changes so it doesn't immediately rerun queries after every selection. That's more useful during report interaction than during construction, but it can help.

One distinction: Pause visuals won't prevent every “Working on it” event. Changes that affect the semantic model—creating/editing DAX measures, changing relationships, model properties, etc.—can still trigger model work. But simple report-layout and visual-formatting operations should become much less painful.

Given that you're currently doing things like renaming visual fields, titles, moving slicers, adjusting formatting, and configuring tooltips, I'd turn Optimize → Pause visuals ON, make a batch of changes, and refresh only when you actually want to inspect the result.

That should save a significant amount of time during the remaining P02 construction.
