This is illustrating the **remaining-balance cap** in the simplified remittance example on Page 2.

Suppose the illustrative terms are:

- **10% of eligible sales** is the remittance share.
- Today's eligible sales would ordinarily produce a **$200 remittance** — e.g., $2,000 of sales × 10% = $200.
- But only **$80 remains** on the repayment balance.

You would not collect the full $200, because that would exceed the remaining amount due. The simplified formula is:

\[
\text{Remittance}=\min(\text{10\% of eligible sales},\text{remaining balance})
\]

So:

\[
\min(\$200,\$80)=\$80
\]

After that $80 remittance, the illustrative remaining balance becomes **$0**.

### Why I put it in the teaching deck

It teaches an important distinction: **the sales-linked percentage determines the provisional remittance, but the outstanding balance caps what can actually be collected in this simplified example.**

So if the normal calculation says $200 but only $80 remains, you collect $80—not $200 and certainly not a negative remaining balance.

The underlying learning guide describes exactly this simplified rule and explicitly notes that it is **illustrative arithmetic, not the R26 remittance generator or a real contract**. [export reference: filecite | turn11file0 | L39-L49]

Looking at the callout by itself, **“$80 remains” is too context-dependent**. A learner could reasonably ask, “$80 of what remains?”

I would make it:

> **THE BALANCE CAP**  
> **Only $80 remains to repay**  
>   
> The sales-share calculation produces $200, but the remaining balance caps this illustrative remittance at **$80**.

That is materially clearer and more pedagogically complete.

## Chapter 1 — What does the engine know about the merchant?

**Business identity and context**

**POS and settlement behavior**

**Deposits and liquidity**  
What cash is actually arriving? What balances/buffers exist? Why is gross sales volume not equivalent to free cash?

**Existing obligations and residual capacity**  
What commitments already consume operating cash? What remains to support financing?

**Operating resilience**  
How does the business respond to stress? What patterns suggest fragility versus resilience?

**Verification / continuity / evidence quality**  
Do we trust and have enough of the data to make the calculation at all?

**Risk and economics**  
What does the comparative risk/loss evidence imply, and does the structure still clear its economic hurdle?

Those aren't generic finance ideas—we already have source-owned Module 1 stages specifically dividing POS, deposits/liquidity, quality, verification/continuity, cash-flow features, capacity, resilience, comparative risk, loss, and economics. [export reference: filecite | turn11file2 | L391-L414]

# Chapter 6 — The seven outcomes should be taught causally

The rejected deck just classified the seven outcomes.

That's useful but shallow.

Instead, show the **driver pattern** behind each outcome.

For example:

**Approve as requested**  
Evidence sufficient + policy permissibility + requested structure feasible + authority satisfied.

**Counteroffer authorized**  
Requested structure fails or is not preferred + permissible alternative passes + authorization satisfied.

**Counteroffer review required**  
Supportable alternative exists + identified governance/judgment gate remains.

**Manual underwriting review**  
Decision remains judgment-dependent beyond automated authority.

**Insufficient evidence decline**

**No feasible structure**  
Evidence is sufficient and no hard stop controls, but governed search finds no permissible structure.

**Policy hard stop**
