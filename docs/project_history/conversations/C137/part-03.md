| What started going off track | How we noticed | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| **Plausible output was being mistaken for correct output.** AI could produce clean code, polished prose, or a convincing analytical explanation that still contained a bad assumption. | Reconciliation, source-to-result checks, reruns, matched before/after comparisons, and independent review exposed differences the narrative alone did not. | Treat every important output as a candidate until it passes validation. **Human acceptance remains explicit.** |
| **Requirements were sometimes too loose.** AI would faithfully implement an interpretation that was reasonable—but not what the business actually intended. | We saw technically coherent outputs fail business or validation tests. One MSBF review, for example, failed because the comparison specification itself was wrong rather than the underlying application rows. | Tighten business intent, constraints, acceptance criteria, and stage boundaries before implementation. Correct the specification, rerun the test, and retain the failed evidence rather than rewriting history. |
| **Context drift accumulated over long projects.** A later AI session could revive an old assumption, obsolete architecture, stale terminology, or superseded decision. | New outputs conflicted with accepted artifacts, physical database state, prior validation, or frozen career evidence. | Establish authoritative sources: frozen baselines, manifests, decision records, accepted schemas, current-state documentation, and explicit supersession rules. |
| **Documentation could outrun implementation.** AI is very good at describing what a system *sounds like* it does. | Comparing architecture and prose against actual code, tables, outputs, and executable state exposed capabilities that were planned, partial, or not yet authorized. | Documentation became conditional on demonstrated state. Distinguish **implemented, validated, configurable, planned, and not authorized**. |
| **Compression caused claim inflation.** Shortening technical work for résumés or executive language sometimes stripped away important boundaries. | Independent audit caught phrases such as raw PD/LGD/EAD or Expected Loss language that could read like production-calibrated banking models rather than synthetic comparative evidence. | Preserve qualifiers at the **point of claim**, not somewhere else on the page. If the qualification makes the sentence cumbersome, remove the unnecessary metric rather than overstate it. |
| **AI naturally optimizes toward completion.** It wants the answer, the finished artifact, the “PASS.” | We found cases where the more valuable result was actually a failure, limitation, or HOLD/PASS_WITH_REVIEW condition. | Preserve negative evidence. A failed test, unresolved assumption, or bounded acceptance can be more valuable than a polished green checkmark. |
| **Recommendation, decision, and authority can blur together.** | In decision-system work, a feasible structure or model recommendation could easily be written as though it were authorization. | Keep **support ≠ authorization**, **signal ≠ action**, and **recommendation ≠ execution** explicit in the architecture. |
| **AI can create cold handoffs at machine speed.** Code might work while the next person cannot explain why, reproduce it, or safely operate it. | Projects became harder to reason about when requirements, logic, validation, and explanation lived in separate places. | My **No Cold Handoffs** principle became even more important: requirements, logic, controls, validation, evidence, interpretation, and ownership travel together. |
| **The development process itself was invisible.** A polished repository could show the destination without showing how decisions were made. | That made it difficult for another reviewer to distinguish my judgment from AI-assisted implementation or inspect the challenge process. | Publish business requirements, validation evidence, version history, and AI conversation records alongside the code. That became **TRUTH made visible**. |
